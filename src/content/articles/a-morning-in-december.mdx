Japan's leadership had planned the entire war around a negotiated settlement. Strike the Pacific Fleet hard enough, secure the territory they needed, then let America come to the table and accept the new arrangement. This wasn't fringe thinking — it was the war plan, from the top down. Even in the final months of 1945, with the war clearly lost, Japanese leaders were still working the angles of a negotiated end, reaching out through Moscow, hoping for terms.

America never offered that table. Once Pearl Harbor happened, unconditional surrender became the only acceptable outcome — not because of the damage done, but because of when it was done. The attack landed while Japanese diplomats were still in Washington, mid-negotiation, talking peace. Roosevelt's "date which will live in infamy" speech doesn't dwell on the casualties. It dwells on the deception — the attack arriving "while... in conversation with its government and its Emperor looking toward the maintenance of peace."

That's the tell. America wasn't just angry about being struck. It was responding to a negotiation being used as cover. Once that happens, trust doesn't get renegotiated — it gets removed. Total occupation, total capitulation, was the only outcome consistent with a broken-trust framework this severe.

Japan's leadership, largely, never modelled this reaction. Their own system resolves conflict through pressure and performance — ostracise the offender, wait for a show of contrition, then let them back in. Applied to America, that expectation became: fight, take losses, then accept the new reality and move on. What they didn't anticipate was a culture with no reinstatement pathway at all. Once a good-faith negotiation gets used as cover, trust isn't repaired by an apology. It's removed. The only acceptable outcome becomes total removal of the offending regime — not readmission on any terms.

One man in the Japanese command did seem to sense it. Isoroku Yamamoto had studied at Harvard, served two postings in Washington, spoke fluent English, played poker with Americans. He opposed the war outright, warning that Japan couldn't win a prolonged fight against American industrial capacity. Whether he specifically foresaw the fury a broken negotiation would trigger, or simply understood American resolve more generally from years of direct exposure, the record doesn't say for certain. But he was the one voice warning the rest of the leadership that this wouldn't play out the way they assumed.

It didn't. And the two unforgivable sins from the last piece are a large part of why.
