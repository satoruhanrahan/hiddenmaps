**Stability isn't the same as working**

Japan's hostility-avoidance strategy held for over two centuries during Sakoku, when the country closed itself off almost entirely from the outside world. But holding steady isn't the same as working well. The period still had recurring famines, a caste system that calcified rather than adjusted, uprisings suppressed rather than addressed. Nothing forced those problems into the open, so they never got corrected. They just got absorbed into "how things are."

That's the real cost of removing competition. A rival doing something better is often the only signal that shows a closed system it's making a mistake. Take that signal away, and errors don't disappear — they just stop being visible. Isolation doesn't trade progress for stability. It trades visible failure for invisible, uncorrected failure.

**Why geography made this the sensible choice for a long time**

This wasn't a strategy anyone sat down and chose over the alternatives. As an island, Japan faced far less of the constant invasion pressure that shaped continental powers — dynastic China, fragmented Europe — where competing with hostile neighbours wasn't optional, it was survival. Without that pressure, the costly, disruptive parts of open competition simply weren't necessary. A system that suppresses internal friction is only a bad trade if friction was buying you something. For most of Japan's history, geography meant it mostly wasn't — so the strategy wasn't being tolerated, it was quietly working, in the narrow sense that nothing punished it.

Centuries of that non-punishment is also why it hardened into something closer to identity than choice. A strategy that gets corrected regularly stays visible as a strategy. One that never gets corrected stops looking like an option and starts looking like the truth.

**Why the option is mostly gone now**

The errors were already there — a country unable to compete economically or technologically, a culture of suppression mistaking stillness for order. Perry's ships in 1853 didn't create any of that. They just removed the isolation that had let it go unchallenged for two centuries. And the door that closed then can't fully close again. Modern Japan depends on imported energy, imported food, export markets, and — with a shrinking working-age population — very likely immigration just to function. Genuine re-isolation now would mean a standard-of-living collapse severe enough to count as exactly the kind of shock that forces a reckoning rather than allowing an escape from one.

What's left, more likely, is a partial version — tighter borders, more insular rhetoric — without the full retreat the strategy actually needs to work the way it once did.
