Robert Axelrod tested this directly in the 1980s, running tournaments that pitted competing strategies against each other in the repeated Prisoner's Dilemma — a game where you can cooperate or betray, over and over, against the same opponent. The strategies that won weren't the cleverest deceivers. They were the honest, reciprocal ones. Cooperate first. Respond in kind. No hidden agenda.

**Why the two curves point in opposite directions**

Deception carries a cost that compounds. Every lie needs maintenance — needs covering, needs to survive contact with a reality that keeps happening whether the lie accounts for it or not. Sooner or later it collides with something the liar didn't control for, and the collision tends to cost more than the original lie ever gained.

Honesty compounds the other way. Each verified interaction lowers the cost of the next one. Trust builds capacity — for faster deals, thinner contracts, less second-guessing. Suspicion, once it has to be actively maintained, is expensive for everyone involved, including the person who caused it.

**Why the old shortcut used to work**

Deception was a viable strategy for most of human history because most encounters were one-off. You could cheat a stranger you'd never see again and walk away clean. That's an anonymous, single-round game — exactly the condition deception performs best under.

That condition barely exists anymore. Credit scores, employment records, online reviews, search history — nearly every modern interaction is effectively part of a long, connected chain, whether the people in it realise it or not. The old shortcut — lie now, it won't come back around — is running in an environment built almost entirely out of repeated rounds now. It's a strategy mismatched to where it's being used.

**The practical version**

None of this makes honesty easy in the moment. It's frequently the harder, slower, less flattering option right when you need the fast one. But the math underneath it doesn't change with the moment — it only ever pays out on a longer clock than most short-term thinking is willing to wait for.

The truth has an old habit of surfacing eventually. Betting against that isn't cynicism. It's just a strategy playing the wrong game.
