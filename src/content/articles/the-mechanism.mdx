This pattern shows up everywhere, in every culture, aimed at different targets. To understand it, you have to look past the stated reason and into the machinery underneath.

**Verdict first, reasons after**

Psychologist Jonathan Haidt ran a study with morally uncomfortable scenarios — no victim, no harm, nothing anyone could point to as wrong. People still condemned them. When pressed for reasons, they struggled, backtracked, contradicted themselves — and kept the verdict anyway. "I don't know why, it's just wrong."

That's the pattern: judgement first, justification bolted on after. Reasoning doesn't produce the verdict. It defends one that already exists.

**Disgust, repurposed**

The feeling underneath a lot of moral outrage traces back to something older — disgust. Originally a biological alarm against contaminated food and disease. Somewhere in cultural evolution, that same circuitry got redirected at ideas, symbols, groups. Flag-burning. Blasphemy. A colleague who won't play along. None of these are toxic. But the brain reacts as if they are.

**What it's actually guarding**

Zoom out further and the function becomes clear. Cooperative groups only work if defection is costly. A member who takes and doesn't give, or worse, who quietly sides with a rival group, can unravel the whole arrangement. A fast, visceral, hard-to-fake punishment response toward that person is a defence — not a bug.

There's an asymmetry that explains why it's so trigger-happy. Wrongly punishing an innocent non-conformist was historically cheap. Wrongly trusting an actual traitor could get the whole group wiped out. Systems built under that kind of asymmetry evolve to over-trigger. Better a smoke alarm that goes off too often than one that stays quiet during the fire.

**The mismatch**

Here's the problem. That system was built for bands of a hundred and fifty people who all knew each other. It's now running inside nations of millions, corporations, courtrooms — environments it was never calibrated for. The alarm still fires. The stakes it's protecting against mostly don't exist anymore. What's left is a very old detector, going off in a very new building.

Everything that follows in this series — how different cultures decide what the unforgivable sin actually is, and what to do once you notice the alarm firing — starts here.
