# The Same Mistake, Twice

*Western society keeps getting other cultures wrong in two opposite directions — and both errors trace back to the same failure of imagination.*

Ask why a country doesn't have Western-style protest movements, or why arranged marriage persists, or why dissent is treated as shameful rather than brave, and you tend to get one of two answers from Western observers. Neither is right, and they fail in mirror-image ways.

## the universalist's mistake

The first answer says: they secretly want what we have. Democracy, individual autonomy, romantic self-determination — these aren't Western preferences, they're human destinations, and any culture that hasn't arrived yet is simply running behind, held back by poverty, authoritarianism, or lack of exposure. Give it time, or give it a push, and it converges.

This was, more or less, the "end of history" assumption of the 1990s. It's a comforting story if you already hold WEIRD values — Western, Educated, Industrialised, Rich, Democratic — because it means you never have to seriously engage with the alternative. There's nothing to argue with, only a timeline to wait out.

But the anthropological record doesn't support it. Joseph Henrich's research popularising the WEIRD acronym makes the opposite point explicit: the individualist, self-expression-oriented psychology common in the modern West is the *outlier*, not the baseline humans are converging toward. Confucian political thought doesn't treat social harmony and deference to legitimate authority as a stage on the way to something better — it treats them as the actual goal, argued for on their own terms, for centuries, by people who understood the alternative and rejected it. Calling that "behind" isn't description. It's a value judgement wearing the costume of a timeline.

## the biologiser's mistake

The second answer runs the opposite direction: they're not behind by choice, they're behind by nature. Somewhere in this line of thinking, difference in values gets quietly reclassified as difference in capacity — a group doesn't have a different, defensible reason for organising life around family and hierarchy rather than individual choice; a group simply *can't* reason its way to anything else.

Franklin Roosevelt is a useful, uncomfortable case study here. Historians including Christopher Thorne and John Dower have documented Roosevelt privately describing the Japanese as biologically underdeveloped — the kind of claim dressed in the language of skull shape and brain development that Samuel Morton had already made fashionable a century earlier, ranking races by cranial capacity with numbers that turned out to reflect his assumptions more than his measurements. This isn't a values disagreement rendered in unfortunate language. It's a false empirical claim, and it functioned exactly the way false empirical claims about biology tend to function: as justification for domination that a values disagreement alone couldn't supply.

## why both fail the same way

These look like opposites — one erases difference, the other manufactures a fake hierarchy out of it — but they share a root. Both let the observer skip the actual work of taking the other culture's stated reasons seriously as an argument.

The universalist skips the work by assuming there's no real argument to engage with — just a delay. The biologiser skips it by assuming the "argument" isn't reasoning at all, just biological output, which forecloses the possibility that the other side ever had a case worth hearing.

Both, in the end, protect the observer from having to sit with an uncomfortable possibility: that another culture examined the same trade-off — say, individual liberty against communal obligation — and reasonably landed somewhere else.

## what's actually left

None of this collapses into "every culture is equally right about everything," which is its own failure, just running in a third direction. Some things aren't really values questions at all. A child dying of a preventable illness is bad by the internal logic of essentially every ethical tradition on Earth — there's no defensible framework, anywhere, that treats child mortality as a legitimate expression of cultural difference. Claims like that hold up cross-culturally because they're not competing visions of the good life; they're close to the one thing that's bad in the same way inside every framework.

But the moment the question shifts from *does this cause suffering* to *how should a good life be organised* — autonomy against hierarchy, individual conscience against collective harmony, tradition against reinvention — the near-universal ground runs out, and what's left is genuine disagreement between coherent, defensible positions.

The honest position sits in that narrow middle, and it takes more discipline to hold than either of the wide, comfortable errors on either side of it: resist collapsing difference into a delay, resist collapsing it into a deficiency, and reserve the small set of judgements that actually cross cultures for the cases where the disagreement isn't really about values at all.
