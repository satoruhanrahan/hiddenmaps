*Japanese work culture is often explained through borrowed English words — "diligence," "loyalty," "harmony." None of them quite capture what's actually happening.*

The real mechanism is older and more familiar than any of that: tribalism.

Not tribalism in the sense of warring clans. Tribalism as the evolved human psychology of belonging — the wiring that makes group membership feel like safety, and exclusion from the group feel like danger. Every human carries this wiring. What differs is how strongly a culture still activates it.

Japan activates it constantly. Centuries of interdependent rice agriculture rewarded tight cooperation between neighbours who couldn't simply leave and start over elsewhere. That history shaped concepts like *uchi-soto* — the sharp line between in-group and out-group — and *gaman*, the quiet endurance of hardship for the sake of the group. Someone who tolerates a brutal work schedule isn't necessarily driven by enthusiasm for the job. They're driven by something closer to identity: in a culture where the self is defined relationally, through the group, losing your place in it doesn't just cost you status. It threatens who you are.

That's also why criticism from an outsider lands so differently than criticism from within. If belonging is where safety comes from, an outsider questioning the group isn't just offering an opinion — they're threatening the thing that keeps everyone safe. The reaction isn't really about the content of what was said. It's about who said it.

Anglo cultures like Australia have largely moved away from this. Settler history rewarded self-reliance and mobility over interdependence, and the tribal wiring atrophied through disuse rather than disappearing. It still exists — sports rivalries and national identity prove that — but it doesn't get triggered in everyday life the way it does in Japan.

This creates a strange position for anyone who has heritage in both worlds. A Japanese person commenting critically on Japan is read as one thing. A foreigner with no connection to Japan is read as another — usually with more slack, because nothing was ever expected of them. But someone who looks and sounds like they should belong, and doesn't orient toward belonging the way the culture expects, doesn't fit either category. They're read as either oblivious to something everyone else senses instinctively, or as quietly rejecting the group on purpose.

Neither reading is comfortable. But both make sense — once you see them as tribalism doing exactly what it evolved to do.
