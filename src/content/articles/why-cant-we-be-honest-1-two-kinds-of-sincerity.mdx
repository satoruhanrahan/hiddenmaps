Lost wallets come back with the cash still inside, and business promises are kept to the letter. So it surprises many Westerners to learn that Japanese culture doesn't treat lying the way they do.

The key word is *makoto*, usually translated "sincerity." To Western ears that sounds like honesty, but it means something different. *Makoto* is purity of heart: loyalty, good intentions, and acting without selfish calculation toward the people you're bound to. It says nothing about whether your words match the facts. Someone can say something untrue and still act with complete *makoto*, if the untruth protects someone's feelings or keeps a relationship intact. There's even a proverb for it: *uso mo hōben*, "a lie can be a useful means."

This points to two different kinds of conscience. A Japanese conscience tends to ask, "Have I let down the people I'm bound to?" A Western conscience tends to ask, "Have I done something wrong in itself?" One protects the relationship; the other protects the principle. Neither culture is made of saints or liars, and Westerners tell white lies about gifts and haircuts like anyone else. But the two sit at opposite ends of a spectrum.

So where did the Western end come from? Much of it traces through Christianity back to the Hebrew Scriptures, which treated truth as sacred. The ninth commandment forbids false witness. Leviticus puts it plainly: "Do not lie to one another." The reason given isn't social convenience but the nature of God: "God is not a man, that he should lie." The Hebrew word *emet* means both truth and faithfulness, so being truthful meant being like God.

Jesus raised the bar further. People in his day swore oaths when they really meant something, which quietly implied that ordinary speech was less reliable. Jesus dismissed the whole system: "Let your yes be yes, and your no be no." Every word should be trustworthy. He then made truth personal: "I am the way, the truth, and the life." Honesty became loyalty to a person, not just a rule. Augustine later concluded that every lie is a sin, even one told to protect someone. Few have lived up to that, but the standard shaped what Western consciences register as wrong.

Which leaves a question: why would a culture treat plain truth as something to soften? The answer lies in what gives a person their worth.
