*The West's tradition of plain speaking rests on a particular idea about people — one it may be losing without fully realising what it loses with it.*

Genesis says humans are made in the image of God, the *imago Dei*. If that's true, a person's worth doesn't come from rank, wealth, or anyone's opinion. It's given by God, equally, to everyone, and nothing anyone says can take it away.

That's what makes honesty safe. If your boss's dignity doesn't depend on face, you can tell him his plan is flawed without destroying him. Truth becomes something you say *to* someone, not something you do *to* them. Christianity added a second ingredient: grace. When someone is shown to be wrong, or does wrong, there's a way back through confession and forgiveness. Failure is real, but it isn't final.

Over the last century, the West has largely let go of the beliefs underneath these ideas. It kept the language of human rights and equal respect, but lost the ground they stood on. Without that ground, worth becomes contingent again. Not on rank this time, but on holding the right opinions.

Cancel culture is what that looks like. Writer Andy Crouch argued in his essay "The Return of Shame" that social media has revived shame culture in the West. Journalist Jon Ronson's *So You've Been Publicly Shamed* shows what it's like on the receiving end: one bad joke, a viral pile-on, a career over. Historian Tom Holland observes that modern progressive moralism still runs on Christian instincts — defending victims and condemning sin — while discarding the theology behind them. What's left is judgement without grace. The sin remains, the outcast remains, but there's no forgiveness and no way back.

The result is a familiar silence. Many Westerners now keep their real views to themselves. They've learned to read the air.

That silence is dangerous. After the 2011 Fukushima nuclear disaster, Japan's own parliamentary commission called it a disaster "made in Japan," blaming a culture of reflexive obedience and reluctance to question authority. A society that can't say hard truths can't fix its mistakes, and the West, amplified by social media, is rebuilding that silence by a different route.

Japan shows that a society can be orderly without plain truth. But a society where people can be honest without fear needs two things together: a dignity no one can take away, and a grace that lets people fail and come back. The West once had both. Whether it can keep them without the faith that gave them is the question it now faces.
